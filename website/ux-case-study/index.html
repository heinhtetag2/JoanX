JoanX rewards children for putting the phone away while they walk

A game for children and a short weekly report for their parents. I designed both apps. The development team built and launched the product from my design.

Left to right: the child's home, the first and last steps of the safety moment, the parent's weekly report and alerts. Screens come from my design prototype. Every number on them is sample data.

What it is
A walking-safety game for children, plus a weekly report for their parents.
Who it is for
Children about 6 to 13, and the adults who look after them.
The problem
Children walk with their eyes on a phone. Locking or tracking them feels like punishment.
How it works
One gentle buzz. More only if they keep going. Never a lock. Stopping earns points.

My role product and UX design, the rules, the design system and the working prototypeClient Joan CompanyBuilt 5 June to 2 October 2026

The problem

Tools for children on phones try to control. JoanX tries to reward.

Korean media call children who walk staring at a phone 스몸비 (“smombies”). My reading is that the usual tools backfire, so JoanX takes a different bet.

What the usual tools do

  • Block the screen
  • Lock the phone on a schedule
  • Track where the child is

These feel like punishment, children work around them, and tracking costs trust between parent and child.

What JoanX does instead

  • Makes walking with the phone put away the thing that wins the game
  • Warns gently, and only as much as needed
  • Shows parents behaviour, never place or content

Gap This is my reasoning, not research. No interviews, surveys or observation of children were done.

  • Never lock the screen.Every warning has to persuade. Calls and texts are never cut off.
  • Detection is a guess.A false alarm has to cost almost nothing, so the first warning is one buzz.
  • No location, no reading messages.The parent report can only describe behaviour.
How it works

One walk to school, in five steps

Only the parent has an account. The child joins with a short code, plays the game, and the parent sees a weekly summary.

The safety moment: each step happens only if the risk continues

  1. Walking + screenThe phone is in use mid-stride.
  2. 10 s graceA chance to put it away unprompted.
  3. One buzzThe screen stays fully usable.
  4. CardIf still risky 2 s later. One action.
  5. MessageThe character speaks. Firmer each round, up to 3 rounds.

At any step, if the child stops, they earn points. If they ignore all three rounds, it is logged for the parent's report and the app stands down, about 80 seconds after it began. The screen is never locked.

What a parent sees, and what never leaves the child's phone

Shared with the parent

  • How long the child walked safely
  • Warnings they got while walking
  • Points and streak
  • Whether protection is on

Never leaves the phone

  • Where the child is
  • Messages and guestbook notes
  • Photos
  • Which apps they use

One shared list in the prototype feeds both apps, so the promise lives in one place.

Key decisions

Five decisions did most of the work

Each one answers a problem that showed up while designing or building. The “now” lines describe what the design does. They are not measured effects on children.

DECISION 1

Warn gently, step by step. Never lock.

Problem
The app has to interrupt a child mid-walk, and its detection can be wrong. A loud or blocking warning gets dismissed or resented.
Decision
A ladder where each step costs a little more attention, and only if the risk continues: buzz, card, character message.
Now
A false alarm costs one vibration. A child who reacts to the buzz never sees a card, and earns more for it.

Found by running it: the message used to vanish in 1.5 seconds, taking the safe button with it. It now stays while the risk lasts. Gap No test with children is recorded.

DECISION 2

Pay for stopping, not for tapping

Problem
The first build paid the moment the child tapped the warning's button. That teaches “trigger, tap, earn”, and the parent's number would count taps.
Decision
Pay only when four phone signals show the child really stopped. Pay more for stopping before any card appears.
Now
Points follow the behaviour, not the tap. A 60-second cooldown and a 300-point daily cap close the obvious farming loop.

Gap The point values are starting guesses, set to be changed from the server. Whether children notice the 50 versus 20 difference is untested.

DECISION 3

Give parents confidence, not surveillance

Problem
A report that shows too much becomes tracking. An urgent alert that looks like every other row gets missed. The first family model could not include a grandparent or a neighbour on the school run.
Decision
A short, fixed list of what parents see. Urgent alerts that stand apart. The child answers “Are you okay?” before anyone is told. Groups instead of a two-parent family.
Now
The report counts behaviour, never place or content. A parent can belong to up to 5 groups, and the child's phone is never touched.

Gap No parent has used the report or alerts in a test.

DECISION 4

Say only what the product does

Problem
The website and store copy had got ahead of the app. For a safety product, one false promise undoes the trust the privacy list is meant to build.
Decision
Check every promise against the running prototype, and rewrite whatever the build does not do.
Now
Seven kinds of untrue claim were rewritten in English and Korean. The privacy list lost a row that was never true.

Gap The audit covered the website and store copy, not every screen. Three contradictions remain inside the app (see Evidence).

BeforeAfter
“Look up” on the website“Put the phone away”. The phone senses walking and screen use, not where a child looks.
Privacy row: “Which app types are blocked”“Whether protection is on”. Blocking only exists in an excluded mode.
DECISION 5

Make the game repeat the message

Problem
A battle game is the most absorbing screen in the app. Built carelessly, the reward for looking away becomes the next reason to stare at the phone.
Decision
Each villain stands for a phone habit, battles are closed while the child walks, and the fight shows safe walking adding to the buddy's power.
Now
The game cannot open mid-stride. The first villain, Ping, is the phone that never stops asking to be checked.

Gap Whether children read the villains as habits, and not only as monsters, is untested.

How it was made

An AI draft on day one, rebuilt over four months

The child's home screen shows the whole process. The first version was generated with AI on 5 June. By October nearly every part had been redesigned.

  • Rules before screensThe warning and reward rules ran as real logic in the prototype. The hardest problems, like paying for taps, only appeared there.
  • The AI wrote most of the code, under my directionEach time a generated screen looked wrong, I wrote the fix down as a rule the AI reads first: flat, one brand green, gold only for points.
  • One design system, kept inside the prototypeA living reference page uses the real components, so new screens stay consistent.
  • The timeline5 June to 2 October 2026, more than 330 commits.
Evidence

What is done, and what is not proven

Launching shows the design could be built and shipped. It does not show that children behave differently or that the warnings are safe to rely on.

Done

Designed and checked

  • Both apps, about 50 screens, in English and Korean
  • The warning and reward rules, as working logic
  • Website and store copy audited against the prototype
  • The development team built and launched the product from this design
Not done

Not tested or measured

  • No usability sessions, interviews or observation
  • No post-launch data yet
  • Every number on the screens is sample data
Expected

Intended, unproven

  • Fewer risky moments over weeks
  • Children accepting warnings more often
  • Parents trusting a narrow report

Known gaps still open

  • The child's help screen still says JoanX can lock the screen, and a parent FAQ says location is used in one mode. The default mode never locks and does not use location.
  • A “Lite” mode that blocks apps is outside this version, but can still be chosen in settings.
  • The website footer still says JoanX has not launched, and the website and apps use different typefaces.

Tests I would run next

  1. Warning comprehension, children 8 to 12. Can a child say what each step wants within two seconds, and which button ends it?
  2. Privacy list, parents. In 90 seconds on the website, can a parent say what they will and will not see?
  3. Field tuning pilot. How often does the buzz fire falsely, and is three rounds too many?
