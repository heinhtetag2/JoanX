JoanX: rewarding children for putting the phone away while they walk

A walking-safety game for children and a deliberately narrow weekly report for the adults who look after them. How I designed it, the decisions that shaped it, and what is still untested.

Try the prototype (opens in a new tab)

Left to right: the child's home, the safety moment's first and last steps, the guardian's weekly report and alerts. Captured from my design prototype; every number on them is sample data.

In one paragraph

Korean media call children who walk with their eyes on a phone 스몸비 (“smombies”). The tools parents have mostly treat it as a control problem: lock the phone, schedule it off, or track the child. My reading is that children learn to work around those tools and that tracking costs trust. JoanX tries a different bet. The phone notices when a child keeps using the screen while walking and gives one gentle buzz. It steps in further only if the child carries on, and it never locks the screen. Walking with the phone put away earns points that grow a buddy character, hatch eggs and power battles against villains. Guardians get a weekly picture of whether the habit is improving, and an urgent alert if a possible fall goes unanswered. They never see location, messages, photos or which apps the child uses.

Product
A child app and a guardian app from one codebase, plus a marketing website
Users
Children aged roughly 6 to 13, and the parents or guardians who install it
My role
Product and UX design of both apps: structure, every screen, the warning and reward rules, the design system, and the working prototype the product was built from. Direction and design of website V2.
Others
Joan Company, the client: the functional spec, the characters and their approval. The development team: the production apps. Myt H Mon: the 17 onboarding story illustrations. Claude, as AI pair programmer: most of the prototype's code, under my direction.
Timeline
5 June to 2 October 2026, 332 commits in the prototype
Status
The development team has launched the product, built from this design. This page has no launch date, store data or usage figures, so it makes no claims about results.

Chapter 2 · Context

The problem

Why the usual answers fail

The problem is 스몸비 (“smombie”): children using a phone while walking near roads. Existing tools treat it as a control problem. They block the screen, lock it on a schedule, or track where the child is. My reading is that these approaches feel like punishment, that children find ways round them, and that tracking costs trust between parent and child.

Gap This is my reasoning, not research. No interviews, surveys or field observation were done, so the whole design rests on reasoning and on building the rules and seeing how they behave. Every UX claim here is reasoned, none is tested.

The product bet

Make walking with the phone put away the thing that wins the game.

Instead of taking the phone away, JoanX makes the safe behaviour pay inside a game the child already cares about. The parent's view stays deliberately narrow, so the product can't turn into surveillance.

Constraints that shaped every screen

ConstraintSourceWhat it forced
Never lock the screenF-10 excludedEvery intervention has to persuade. None of them can block, so calls and texts are never cut off.
Detection is a guessF-03, F-04, F-30 (90% accuracy is a target)A false alarm must cost the child very little, so the first signal is one buzz.
No location, no message readingF-05, F-06 excludedThe guardian report can only describe behaviour, never place or content.
Client-owned charactersJoan Company approves names, stories and artArt and names change at the client's pace, so the UI had to survive art swaps.

How success was defined

The spec defines success through the guardian's eyes: a reduction rate in risky moments against the child's own earlier behaviour, how often warnings are accepted, and growth in safe walking time (F-20). Detection accuracy has a target of 90% or more during a tuning period (F-30). No figures for any of these exist yet, so this case study can describe what the design does, but not whether it works.

Chapter 3 · Context

How JoanX works

Before the decisions, the product itself: what a family does with it, how the two apps are organised, the one sequence everything hangs off, and the line the guardian app never crosses.

One walk to school

  1. The guardian sets up. Only the guardian has an account. They add the child and get a six-digit code that works for five minutes.
  2. The child joins. The child watches a short illustrated story, types the code, hatches a starter egg (their first buddy, Lumi), and grants four phone permissions one at a time. A refused permission limits protection instead of stopping it.
  3. The walk. Home says “You're protected”. If the child keeps using the screen while walking, the safety moment runs: one buzz first, more only if they carry on. Stopping earns points. Ignoring is recorded. The phone is never locked.
  4. After school. Points buy XP for the buddy, eggs and outfits. Battles against villains open once the child has stopped walking.
  5. That week, the guardian. The report shows how often the child stopped when warned, how often they ignored it, and safe walking time against last week. A possible fall that the child doesn't answer within 20 seconds alerts the guardian at once.

Everything in the product hangs off one sequence. It is a rule engine more than a screen, so I mapped it first.

Drawn in seconds, the ladder shows how patient it is. If a child ignores everything, JoanX spends about 80 seconds over three rounds asking, then records it and stops. Chapter 4 · The product

Five key decisions

Five decisions carried the most weight. Each follows the same shape: what the person was trying to do, what made it hard, what evidence pointed at the problem, and what changed.

A. Interrupting a child without punishing them

Problem

The app has to interrupt a child in the middle of a walk, and its detection is a guess. A loud or blocking interruption gets dismissed or resented, and a false alarm teaches the child to ignore the app.

Constraints

The screen is never locked (F-10 excluded), so the child can always call or text. The spec asks for a message covering about 20% of the screen (F-09).

Decision

A ladder in which each step costs the child a little more attention, and only if the risk continues: one buzz, a two-second wait, a card with one action, then a character message that grows firmer over up to three rounds.

Why

A false alarm at the first step costs one vibration. A child who reacts to the buzz never sees a card, and earns more for it (decision B).

Evidence and limits

The ladder comes from the spec (F-08, F-09). Its readability problems were found by running it in the prototype and are recorded in commits (below). Gap No test with children is recorded.

Choosing the ladder was the easy part. Most of the work was making each step readable and fair, and five problems only showed up once it ran:

  • The message vanished before it could be read. "It faded the whole sheet — mascot, copy and both buttons — on the message cycle, so a line was gone in 1.5s and the safety CTA went with it." The sheet now holds while the risk lasts and only the words cross-fade (791ef69). The hold was later lengthened to 4 seconds. Gap That differs from the spec's 1.5 s, and no client approval is recorded.
  • Dismiss looked like the safe action. The dismiss pill was the same colour as its backdrop and read as bare text, and it should not look equal to stopping: "it acknowledges the warning, it does not end the risk" (2aa2e6e).
  • The reward was a second interruption. A celebration pop-up after the child stopped was removed: "The modal was a second interruption tacked onto the one we had just resolved." Points still bank silently (35b6881).
  • The words were ambiguous. "I looked up" also means looking something up in a list, so the copy moved away from it (46c123f). The Korean side got the opposite fix: adult words like 방명록 and 반영됐어요 were replaced with words an 11-year-old uses.
  • The scolding voice. In September the warnings switched from the child's own buddy to King Cubix, a narrator character with a pose for each tone (0c7ebf5). Permission requests followed because "permissions are the app's safety voice, not the pet's" (5a2799e). Interpretation For the warnings, no reason is recorded. My reading is the same one: the buddy stays a friend and never scolds. This conflicts with spec F-08.5, which expects the child's character to speak.

Alternatives, still switchable in the prototype

I built seven warning layouts (Spotlight, Card, Frame, Signal, Strip, Sheet and Banner) and two narrators so they could be compared live on a phone. Spotlight with King Cubix is the default.

Still unproven. Whether a child reads and acts on the card inside two seconds, whether three rounds is too many, and how often the buzz fires falsely. These need the field tuning period the spec plans (F-30) and a test with children.

B. Paying for safety, not for taps

Problem

In the first build the stop bonus landed the moment the child pressed the warning's button. A child could learn “trigger, tap, earn”, and the guardian's “stopped when warned” number would be counting button presses.

Constraints

Points are the only currency. The phone can only sense walking, screen state and phone use, so “stopped” has to be defined from those.

Decision

Pay only when four signals hold, and pay more for stopping before any card appears (+50) than after one (+20). Add a 60-second cooldown and a 300-point daily cap (69504ff, 20 July).

Why

Paying “warning, then stop” as much as never needing a warning would teach a child to earn a warning.

Evidence and limits

Found while building the rule engine: the product was paying for a tap and calling it safety. Gap The original flat +20 bonus and the built 50/20 tiers are both still written down in the project files.

The tiering is the subtle part. Paying the "warning, then stop" loop as much as never needing a warning would teach a child to earn a warning. The code comment states it plainly: "Never reward the warning→stop loop as much as never needing the warning."

Two earlier changes kept the economy legible. A second invented currency ("coins") was folded into points to match the spec (a06fc2c, 30 June). And gold was reserved for points and XP alone. On 1 September the buddy's Add-XP button went through thirteen versions in one day, from solid gold to outline to no colour, and ended as a green fill, because "the buddy's individual color (e.g. Munch's orange) could clash" (ef32279).

Still unproven. The point values are guesses that the code makes server-configurable on purpose. Whether children notice the 50/20 difference, and whether it changes behaviour, needs data.

C. Giving parents confidence without surveillance

Problem

A guardian wants to know whether their child is getting safer, and to hear at once if something serious happens. A report that shows too much becomes tracking, a feed where every row looks alike buries the alert that matters, and the first family model could not hold the adults who actually share a school run.

Constraints

No location tracking and no reading of messages (F-05, F-06 excluded). Fall detection, like walking detection, can be wrong.

Decision

A fixed, short list of what guardians see, kept in one place in the code and published on the website. Urgent alerts styled apart from every other row. The child answers a possible fall before anyone is told. Guardian groups instead of a two-parent family.

Why

A narrow report that guardians can read in full is easier to trust than a dashboard, and an urgent alert only works if it survives a glance.

Evidence and limits

Three problems, each found by running the prototype's own flows and recorded in commits (below). Gap No guardian has used the report or the alerts in a test.

  • The urgent alert looked like every other row. "An impact alert — a possible fall, no response for twenty seconds — read exactly like a scroll warning three rows down, and its message was clipped at 'please check right a...'." The child's mascot on each row "blunted the one signal that had to survive a glance." Urgent rows now get a red rule and title and a call link in the row itself, and the tab shows an unread count (ce71daa, 21 September).
  • The family model couldn't hold real families. In July, a second parent had to re-scan the child's phone, which unlinked the first device. Two equal parents fixed that (791ef69). By September the two-parent family was the limit: "The people who actually share a school run — an aunt, a grandparent, a neighbour on Tuesdays — could not be expressed at all." Groups replaced it. A guardian can belong to several groups, joins by QR and needs an admin's approval. The child's phone is never touched (ef672b4). The Korean label is 그룹, not 가족, because the people in one are often not a family.
  • The fall check asks the child first. Impact detection is a guess, so the child sees "Are you okay?" with a 20-second countdown before anyone is told. "I'm okay" ends it with no alert.

Still unproven. Whether guardians find a behaviour-only report enough, whether they trust a list they can't verify, and how often fall detection fires falsely. None of it has been put in front of a guardian.

D. Saying only what the product does

Problem

The website and store copy had drifted ahead of the product. For a safety app, one false promise undoes the trust the privacy list is meant to build.

Constraints

The copy exists in English, Korean and, on the website, Mongolian. What the product does is set by the spec and the code, not by the copy.

Decision

Audit every promise on the website and store listing against the running prototype, and rewrite anything the build doesn't do.

Why

A guardian deciding whether to install a safety app is mostly deciding whether to believe it.

Evidence and limits

The audit (25 September), a correction to the privacy list (18 September) and a copy pass on “look up” (1 October), all in commits. Gap Some help text inside the app still contradicts the build (below).

The audit found seven kinds of false promise: "no location data at all, a single buzz that never repeats, pairing by scanning a code, groups with no codes, points that unlock villains, reports measured against the child's first week, and a privacy list shown on the child's profile." Each line was rewritten to match the build in English and Korean (e4e1db6).

The privacy list itself had a row that was never true: "The 'Which app types are blocked' row was never true of the shipped app: blocking only exists in Lite mode." It became "Whether protection is on", and "Which apps you use" moved to the list nobody sees (9671f64). Finally, the website said "look up" throughout, which implies the phone can tell where a child is looking. It can't; it senses walking and screen use. The copy now says "put the phone away" (1a29014).

Still inconsistent in the prototype. The audit covered the website and store copy, not every screen in the app. Three contradictions remain inside the app: the child's help screen still answers “Why did my screen lock?” with “JoanX locks the screen…”; a guardian FAQ says location is used in Smart mode; and Lite mode, which the spec excludes, can still be chosen in settings and does show a full-screen block. The default mode, Smart, never locks. These are open work, listed in chapter 10.

E. A game world built from the phone's own habits

Problem

The child needs something worth spending points on, but a battle game is the most absorbing screen in the app. Built carelessly, the reward for looking away from the phone becomes the next reason to stare at it, possibly mid-stride.

Constraints

The spec sets five battles a day against a ladder of villains (A-8). The client owns the characters and approves villain names, stories and art. No PvP, no chat, no paid items (A-11).

Decision

Make each villain one of the phone's own habits, close battles while the child is walking, and show inside the fight that safe walking is what wins.

Why

The game should repeat the product's message, not compete with it.

Evidence and limits

The walking rule and its reason are written into the code (canChallenge); the battle sequence was reworked across six commits in July and August. Gap Whether children read the villains as habits is untested.

Three choices keep the game on the product's side:

  • The villains are the phone's habits. Each of the ten villains on the road stands for one way a phone holds attention, and its battle ability works the same way. Ping is notifications, "the phone that never stops asking to be checked," and its ability means you lose the first move because a glance is all it needs. The ladder climbs from there to the final boss, Vilord: "the moment the phone stops being a tool and starts being in charge."
  • Battles close while the child walks. The rule sits first among the gates, ahead of locked villains or tries left: "A battle is the most absorbing screen in the app, so it is the LAST thing that may open mid-stride." The copy already promised that battles pause while walking; this rule makes the sentence true (F-19, canChallenge in data.jsx).
  • The fight says the product's sentence. The first versus screen lasted 1.8 seconds: VS appeared, a second passed, the result arrived. It became three beats. In the middle one, the buddy's power counts up through the safe-walking bonus, so the link between walking safely and winning is shown "at the one second a child is leaning in" (5ac4c49). The exchange was built as "a contest, not an execution", so the loser still lands blows, and a tap skips ahead.

How hard each villain is, and why safe walking is what moves a child up the road

Villain power climbs 15 points a level, then jumps for the two bosses. The buddy's power comes from its stats, which grow with XP bought with safe-walking points, and a battle adds a safe-walking bonus to the odds (BATTLE_ODDS). Walking safely is what moves a child up the road.

Smaller decisions support these. The villain map uses sticker-style markers, and state is shown by a pulse ring on the current challenger, "never by recolouring the outline" (c985a37). Each villain card shows the tries left today, so the daily limit is visible before the fight, not enforced as a surprise after tapping Start (29a1497). Villains unlock strictly in order, and no villain starts defeated: a new player meets Ping first (9671f64).

The fight, and what a loss still gives

Once the child has stopped walking, a battle is three short steps: pick a fighter, watch the clash, read the result. The result shows the battle math (the stats behind the buddy's power and the win chance), so a loss reads as a close roll rather than an unfair one. Losing still pays 10 points for trying.

Still unproven. Whether children read the villains as phone habits or only as monsters, whether the night-time villain art is too dark for the youngest children, and whether five battles of about six seconds each pulls attention back toward the screen. Gap The villain roster exists in three versions across the spec, the checklist and the build; this page uses the build's names.

Chapter 5 · The product

Set-up, rewards and rooms

The decisions above cover the safety moment, rewards, the guardian's view and the game. This chapter covers the rest of the product: how a family gets set up, and what a child spends points on. These screens carried less risk than the safety moment, but most of a child's time in the app is spent in them.

Set-up, guardian: signing in

Only the guardian has an account. The first screen offers three doors, so nobody has to guess which one is theirs: a new account, logging back in, or joining a group someone else already runs. Consent comes before the account, because the guardian agrees on the child's behalf.

Pairing: the child joins with a code, not a password

A child never makes an account or remembers a password. The guardian's app shows a six-digit code that lasts five minutes; the child types it in (or scans a QR instead), and the two phones are linked. Then King Cubix asks for the four permissions one at a time, each with the reason in a child's words. A refused permission is allowed: protection runs limited and says why, instead of failing.

Inviting another guardian

The school run is rarely one parent. A guardian can invite others into a group with a QR or a code, and the person joining sees exactly which children they would see before they ask. Nothing is shared until the group's admin accepts.

AThe admin
BThe guardian joining

A guardian can belong to up to five groups. The child's phone is never involved in joining a group.

What a guardian can control

Each child has one page of rules and settings. Its top half answers “is protection working?” (the groups, the device, and every permission the child accepted at setup). Its bottom half holds the choices a guardian can actually make.

  • Protection modeSmart, the default and the mode in scope: warnings plus the game. Lite (not in this revision): blocks chosen kinds of apps while walking, calls always allowed.
  • Warning sensitivityGentle, Balanced or Strict, on one slider.
  • Time rulesStricter for the school commute, calmer at home, as many schedules as a family needs.
  • Game and rewardsA switch for families who want the warnings without the game.
  • DeviceReconnect after a new phone, by scanning a new QR.
  • Remove childAt the bottom, in red, away from everything else.

Gap Lite blocks apps while walking, but the website and the safety moment promise that JoanX never locks the phone. The prototype's code marks Lite as excluded from this revision, yet the settings screen still offers it. Either hide it until it ships, or give it a name and description that don't contradict the promise. The “At home” rule is also described as geofenced, a location claim the product otherwise avoids.

After school: eggs and the shop

Eggs are how a child gets a new buddy. Most are earned by walking safely: clearing every daily mission gives a Common Egg, every weekly mission a Rare Egg, and walking milestones such as 10 km add more. The shop sells the same eggs for points, and an egg already earned shows its price beside it, so a child can see what it was worth.

Dressing the buddy

Outfits are the most personal thing points can buy, so dressing Lumi got its own screen. Tapping an item puts it on straight away as a try-on. A bar at the bottom names the item and offers Buy or ×, so nothing is paid for by accident (17f290e).

How the outfit pictures were made

Before the screen existed, I made the clothes themselves. Lumi isn't built from layers. Each outfit combination is its own complete picture, which I generated with AI and laid out on a Figma board, then the app swaps to the matching picture. An animated, rigged character wasn't in the budget. Whole pictures were, and they look finished on day one.

A rigged character in Rive (or a similar tool) would turn that into one new layer, and add the life a still picture can't: idle breathing, a reaction to a new outfit, a cheer after a safe walk.

Designing the Dream Room

Buddies live in rooms, and the profile friends visit opens on the Dream Room. I designed it in Figma in three steps: an empty room as the base, decorated previews to find where pieces belong, then the pieces themselves as separate cut-out items the child places.

  1. 1Empty room first. The background carries the mood on its own (window, clouds, shelves), so a room with nothing bought still looks finished.
  2. 2Preview, then mark the spots. Decorated versions showed where pieces read well: an ornament hanging top-left, a cabinet on the left wall, an armchair on the right, a rug on the floor.
  3. 3One piece per spot, picked from three. Each spot became its own catalogue slot with its own button, so all four can be in the room at once. The flower rug, flower armchair, crescent moon and mushroom cabinet shipped (79300a8).

Gap Pieces don't layer around the buddy yet: dropped at its default spot, the armchair or rug covers Lumi, so these were dragged clear by hand. Depth sorting by floor position would fix it.

Chapter 6 · The product

The website

JoanX is two things a family meets: the apps, and the website that decides whether the apps get installed at all. The website had its own brief and its own design problem.

A guardian, a phone, ninety seconds

The brief (16 September) names the primary reader: a guardian of a 6 to 13-year-old, on a phone, deciding in about ninety seconds whether JoanX is worth installing. They arrive suspicious of two things: that it is another blocker their child will defeat, and that it will spy. (The client, partners and pilot schools are secondary readers.) The page has one job: to move that guardian from “another parental-control app” to “this is a game my child would keep, and it doesn't watch them.”

From a one-day AI draft to a designed page

V1 was generated by AI in one day from prompts (16 September), and it is kept as a baseline. V2 is my design: I stripped the page back to a plain base on 25 September and rebuilt it section by section over the following week, using AI to build what I directed. Both are still live: open either below, or use the switch on the site to move between them. Shown here in Korean, the main market; each site has its own language switch.

A page ordered by a parent's questions

The V2 rebuild started by taking everything away. The first step kept the same sections and copy but “no gradients, shadows, icon tiles, pills, tilted phones or mascot”, and marked each section so it could be redesigned one at a time (b4bb14b). The next made “each section's label the parent's question” and its heading the plain answer, in the order a parent asks (a2d48d2). Only then did the visual design begin.

Carrying the app's brand onto the web

Adding motion, scroll by scroll

The apps are flat, so the website's motion had to earn its place: it is used only where it tells the story. I directed it one section at a time and built it with AI, without an animation library. Each moment is a section that pins in place while you scroll, and the scroll position drives every step. The reader sets the speed, can stop at any point, and can scroll back to replay it.

Visual language: flat, one green

Chapter 8 · How it was made

Design system and AI

Two apps, one designer and an AI pair programmer make a fast team, and a risky one. The AI writes the code it's asked for quickly, but left alone it drifts toward a generic look: glows, sparkles, pills on everything. Keeping the product consistent meant treating the design system as two things. It is a set of tokens and components the code is built from, and a set of written rules the AI reads before it builds anything.

Three tiers, one source

Every value flows through three tiers, and a token's name says which tier it is. Product code only uses the semantic or component tier, never a raw colour. That rule is why the brand could move from ocean to pink to green without editing screen by screen.

A living reference, inside the prototype

The system is documented as a page in the prototype itself: 20 sections, from colour and type to fields and sheets, characters, motion and sound, with live components to try and code to copy. The written spec (DESIGN-SYSTEM.md) and colour galleries sit beside it. Because the page renders the real components, a developer sees exactly what ships.

Teaching the AI the house style

When a generated screen looked wrong, I didn't just fix that screen. I named what was wrong, wrote the fix down as a short rule with its reason, and saved it where the AI reads it at the start of every session. Seventeen design and copy rules were saved this way. Many also went into the code as comments, so anyone reading the component sees the reason too.

Keeping the reference honest

A reference the AI trusts has to stay true. On 2 October the design system page was checked line by line against the code. It had last been updated on 21 August, 175 commits earlier, and it had drifted:

  • It still documented glow shadows for buttons that no longer used them, and one demo used a colour token that didn't exist.
  • It described an input with an icon option that had been removed, and a label style that had become a floating label.
  • It said the child app's accent was ocean, when both apps had used one brand green since July.
  • It counted the tab bars wrong and claimed the translation helper could fill in numbers, which it can't.
  • It had no section at all for fields and bottom sheets, the narrator, the villains or sound.

Everything was corrected in one pass, and the missing sections were added (66708c6). The audit also turned up things in the app itself to fix later, such as an icon whose image file is missing. The lesson: docs drift as fast as the product moves. The rules the AI follows are only as good as the reference they point to.

Chapter 10 · Results

Reflection and next steps

What I'd keep

  • Rules before screens. The hardest problems (paying for taps, the vanishing message) only appeared once the spec ran as real logic.
  • Write the reason next to the change. Most decisions in this case study can be quoted because the reason went into the commit or the code.
  • Write taste down. Working with an AI went fastest when a correction became a saved rule with a reason, not a one-off fix.
  • Audit the promise. For a safety product, checking the copy against the build mattered as much as any layout.

What I'd do differently

  • Fewer variants, decided sooner. Some areas still have 10 to 24 alternatives. That helped the client choose live, but it also left old options in the code.
  • Put children in front of it earlier. Every UX claim here is reasoned; none is tested.
  • One source of truth for docs. Several documents now disagree with the build: villain names, battles per day, bonus values, guardian sign-in and child age.

With more time, what I'd fix

These are real inconsistencies found while building and auditing the product, not hypotheticals. Brand alignment comes first, because it's the most visible to parents and the one the AI drifts on most easily.

  2. Privacy list, parents. In 90 seconds on the website, can a parent say what they will and won't see? Do they trust it?
